Epidural Ninja — Force-Based Bone and Epidural Space Identification for Robotic Epidural Needle Insertion

QUT — robotics / medical device project

Problem. Epidural anaesthesia requires a needle to cross the posterior spinal ligaments and stop in a fat-filled space only a few millimetres wide, just before the dura. Clinicians do this almost entirely by feel: bone is recognised as a hard stop, and the epidural space as a sudden loss of resistance (LOR) on a syringe plunger. This project investigates whether a general-purpose robot arm can make the same two judgements without any visual input.

Objectives.

Identify bone contact without vision.
Identify when the needle tip reaches the epidural space.

Work done.

Wrote a Python control stack for the open-source 7-DoF OpenArm (CAN-FD motor interface, inverse kinematics with tool-axis constraints, straight-line needle descents, and logging).
Conducted a focused review of 20 full-text papers and eight supplementary studies covering the manual procedure, sensing methods, non-visual bone identification, robots for similar procedures, and evaluation metrics.

Results — Objective 1 (bone detection).

The arm's own joint torques could not distinguish the printed vertebra from silicone: after contact, joint 2 varied only between −1.15 and −0.99 N·m, and the silicone run produced larger torque swings than the hard run on five of seven joints.
A redesigned syringe holder with a 1 kg bar load cell in the needle load path separated the printed vertebra from silicone in all 26 insertions (peaks of 2.11 M counts vs 3.38 M counts; Welch p = 1.7 × 10⁻⁹).

Results — Objective 2 (epidural space detection).

A force-sensitive resistor on the plunger was not sensitive enough at low force.
A 100 g plunger load cell separated a tip occluded by silicone from a tip in the epidural space tube in all 25 trials. The median plunger force over the second half of the stroke separated the classes by 4.68 M counts, with 100% cross-validated accuracy.

Conclusion. Both judgements can be made from force alone when the sensor sits directly in the relevant load path; relying on the robot's motor torques is not sufficient. Values are uncalibrated raw counts from a lumbar phantom. Next steps: calibration, closed-loop testing on the arm, and layered anatomical phantoms.

Keywords: Medical robotics, force sensing, load cells, loss of resistance, OpenArm, CAN-FD, inverse kinematics, Python
